Second of July, 2026 Engine Deep Dive

Unreal’s Gameplay Ability System — Thinking in Abilities and Attributes

The first time you open the Gameplay Ability System, it feels like stepping into a cathedral. Vaulted ceilings. Echoes. Architecture that demands you slow down and look up. GAS is not a toolkit so much as a philosophy — a way of thinking about game logic as a conversation between abilities, attributes, and gameplay tags. And like any good conversation, the hard part isn’t speaking. It’s learning the grammar.

Most developers coming from Unity or Godot are used to MonoBehaviour-style scripting: attach a component, write an Update loop, fire a raycast. GAS asks you to unlearn that. You stop thinking about frames and start thinking about predicates. Can the character attack? That’s not an if-statement. That’s a GameplayTag query evaluated by an Ability System Component that may or may not be replicating across the network. The question is not “is the button pressed” but “does the world permit this action.”

Understanding GAS is less like learning an API and more like learning to think in a new grammar — one where every action is a conversation between abilities, attributes, and gameplay tags.

I spent three weeks just reading the source. Not the docs — the source. AbilitySystemComponent.cpp is over eight thousand lines. You don’t read it. You live inside it for a while, like a cabin in winter, until the shape of it becomes familiar. And then one morning you wake up and realize: this isn’t an API. This is a worldview. Every action is a prediction. Every attribute change is a story.

Eighteenth of June, 2026

From Unity C# to Unreal C++ — A Month of Unlearning

The first thing you notice is the silence. In Unity, C# holds your hand. Garbage collection hums quietly in the background. Null-reference exceptions are polite. Unreal C++ does not hold your hand. It hands you a macro — UCLASS() — and waits to see what you do with it.

I shipped three Unity titles before touching Unreal. Two mobile games and a small Steam release. I felt competent. The kind of competent where you forget how much scaffolding your tools have been quietly providing. Unreal strips that scaffolding away and replaces it with something older and stranger: a preprocessor that rewrites your headers before the compiler ever sees them. A garbage collector that only cares about UObject descendants. A reflection system that makes C# feel understated.

The hardest lesson was not technical. It was humility. I had to accept that knowing how to make games did not mean knowing how to make games this way. The engine is older than I am in some respects. It carries decisions made in 1998. And yet — and this is what kept me going — those decisions have a coherence to them. A stubborn, architectural coherence. Once you stop fighting the grain and start reading along it, the whole thing opens up.

Fourth of June, 2026

Building Responsive Combat with Enhanced Input and GAS

There is a moment in every combat system where the design document meets the codebase and something has to give. The designer wants the light attack to cancel into the heavy attack, but only during the first twelve frames. The engineer wants to know what “cancel” means in a networked environment with server reconciliation. Bridging that gap is what the Enhanced Input system and GAS were built to do — but the bridge itself is something you have to construct.

// Binding Enhanced Input to Gameplay Ability activation void ACastielCharacter::SetupPlayerInputComponent( UInputComponent* PlayerInputComponent) { Super::SetupPlayerInputComponent(PlayerInputComponent); if (auto* EI = Cast<UEnhancedInputComponent>( PlayerInputComponent)) { if (AbilitySystemComponent && InputConfig) { EI->BindAction(InputConfig->LightAttackAction, ETriggerEvent::Started, this, &ACastielCharacter::OnLightAttackStarted); EI->BindAction(InputConfig->HeavyAttackAction, ETriggerEvent::Triggered, this, &ACastielCharacter::OnHeavyAttackTriggered); } } }

The elegance of this approach is that the input layer and the ability layer remain completely decoupled. Enhanced Input handles the hardware — triggers, chorded actions, held vs. tapped — while GAS handles the game logic. They meet only at the binding point, which becomes the single place you need to debug when something goes wrong. And something always goes wrong, because combat systems are fundamentally about timing, and timing is the hardest thing to get right across a network.

What surprised me most was how much of the work was not in writing code but in designing the tags. Ability.Attack.Light, State.Combat.InCombat, Cooldown.Attack.Light — the tag hierarchy is the design. Get it right and your combat feels like a conversation. Get it wrong and you spend three days debugging an interaction between a dodge roll and a health potion.
